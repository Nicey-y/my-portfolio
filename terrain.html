TERRAIN

TERRAIN is a Melbourne-based start-up. They are a bookshop, a gallery, and a working studio, while also offer environmental and consulting services. I first came into contact with them in 2025 through University of Melbourne's capstone subject, where I worked in a 5-people team as a Scrum Master and Full Stack Developer to help TERRAIN fixing and managing their current website, plus fixing their online bookshop to finally get it live and generating revenue.

To understand why I became attached to TERRAIN, I'd like to explain their mission. TERRAIN acts to bridge the gap between human and the non-human world, because a lot of the problems in our current climate stem from us living in a human-centric world and thus having disconnected from the surrounding environment as a whole species, while ironically also relying on the Earth's resources to live. TERRAIN's solution to this crisis comes through their bookshop that hosts critical conversations about this specific problem throughout fields such as ecology, technology, design,...etc.

As a technology enthusiast, I connect to TERRAIN's mission on a personal level as I side with their stand on technology specifically. I believe that everyone can own their tech stack and only through that can they be aware of the impact of their digital life and act upon it.

Participation in taking the membership program off the ground

As a result, I came back to TERRAIN in April 2026 and alongside Cristina Napoleone - the founder of TERRAIN - helped design the technological/digital stack behind TERRAIN's newest membership program that launched May 2026. It was a lot between juggling 4 university subjects and working on a tight deadline, but I also learnt a lot, from how much it needs to keep a business running to how much burden technology and alleviate from a business' shoulders.

Automatic Desk-Booking System

For context, as a member of TERRAIN, you can book a desk in the working studio. A month into operation, I realised that instead of manually checking the calendar and approving bookings, the whole process and be automated. I pitched this to Cristina and she gave me the green light, for which I am truly grateful for. Because as a fresh graduate, a real product that is used by real people is much more valuable than a spin-up project that lives only on a Github repo. Cristina has trusted me with the TERRAIN's digital infrastructure and customers. It is an honour, and a heavy responsibility reminding me that I have to always keep my performance at its peak.

Since the code base itself is under TERRAIN's intellectual property agreement, I cannot share the Github repo, therefore I will outline my design and building process down below. If you are a recruiter/hiring manager reading this, I'm happy to talk into more details in person, or provide more evidence after getting an approval from TERRAIN.

Phase 1: Designing

I did my own research and the result is that a PostgreSQL/Supabase backend, with a FastAPI as a backend to connect it with the current Aitable-base production is the best mediation. TERRAIN admin processes still heavily relies on Airtable for its interactive UI. In theory I can also just make a replica of its UI as part of this project, but after some consideration, I've decided to shelve that as a future update since an Airtable-level UI would have taken me too long, whereas my immediate goal is to get a system running and taking off some admin burden as soon as possible.

Phase 2: Learning

Not sure if this goes without saying so I'd like to admit up front that I don't know the full tech stack at the beginning of this project. I don't think of that as hinderance but instead, I treat this as an opportunity to learn new things. Before this, I took a course in uni about database, and have been using Python extensively, so learning PostgreSQL and FastAPI will be a level-up from my current skill level. Naturally I thought it would be easy. Not like I'm learning something from scratch, right?

Well, I was wrong.

In the uni database course, we learnt database theories plus some basic SQL. The theories are very useful when it comes to designing the actual schema and deciding how the tables relate to each other to achieve my goal, but I found myself lacking in every way when it comes to writing practical PostgreSQL code. That was when I realise I have to learn PostgreSQL almost entirely from the beginning. However, I am on a short deadline, so I turned to Claude to build me interactive exercises from which I can both freshen-up my rusty SQL, and learn new things like pgplsql functions and RLS.

It took me a week of relentless studying and writing PostgreSQL functions, to a point that I can write a function in my dream the same way with a pen and paper. Regardless, that was a successful operation.

Phase 3: Building

It won't be a surprise but I will say it anyway: this stage takes the majority of my time and is the most gruelsome battle I've ever had with Python. Despite being the programming language that I'm most familiar with, there were moments that I really just wanted to go back to C and be a caveman. To make up for how writing code is as natural as breathing air and learning FastAPI on-the-run is easy, debugging was truly the level-100 final boss. Looking at error messages and tracing lines of code was not enough anymore, which urged me to look into a debugging tool that is beginner friendly. From my experience with C, I've came to love gdb, so I search for something similar for Python and there actually is one! And it's even named pdb. Sweet!

Phase 4: Testing

If I skip this step I may as well be doomed. I cannot bring myself to ship an untested product while writing test cases in Python is so convenient already. I used pytest and wrote close to 70 automated test cases, wired them up to Github Actions so that they automatically run on push to a test branch. I divided the tests into 3 layers: pure logic layer, database communication layer, and manual tests that needs to be performed by a human tester (me).

Phase 5: Deployment

This booking system's backend needs to be hosted on a machine. Now it's worth mentioning again that TERRAIN is a startup, so their budget isn't exactly redundant. We are already paying a monthly subscription fee to Airtable, and adding another hosting fee on top of that is not ideal. I thought that since we are still at the beginning phase of the membership program, why not host the backend on my second laptop at home? The traffic is low enough, and that laptop is currently only used to host a Discord bot I made, so adding another low-traffic service like a Python script wouldn't be a big deal. I consulted Cristina and she also agreed with this plan, because we also have a project on the horizon that would solve this hosting issue either way.

I hosted the booking backend on my second laptop at home. I set up SSH tunnel to have control over it remotely from my main laptop. The backend is configured into a systemd service on Linux Ubuntu that allows me to start, stop, restart, and inspect its output through remote command line actions.
